Product and users

Half a page, anonymised. This file goes into the AI steps first, so write it for a reader who knows nothing about the project.

The product

Grab's food delivery in Vietnam lets people find food and drinks from restaurants and have them delivered to their door, so they do not need to go out to buy them. It is one part of Grab's larger ecosystem of services, and this project looks only at the food delivery journey.

The users

Anyone aged about 18 to 50: students, people at work, and retired people. Ordering food is a common, everyday need, so the owner does not limit the group by age. This is the owner's working assumption, not a research finding.

Their main goal is to reach restaurants that are not near them and to get food without leaving home. It suits people who are busy with work and have no time to cook. Typical situations are dinner at home and food late at night when no one wants to cook.

Where it stands now

The owner has used the app in Vietnam on an iPhone, with the English interface (app version v33437965), and has taken screenshots of the whole journey from end to end: discovering the app, choosing a restaurant, picking the food, paying, and tracking the delivery. These screens have not been analysed yet.

What success might look like

assumed: the owner's first ideas, to be tested by the research.

  • Users can explore restaurants, not only the ones they already know.
  • New restaurants can reach users more easily, and users find it easy to try a restaurant once or become loyal to it.
  • Payment is smooth: little manual input, one-time payment, quick connection to a payment method.
  • Tracking the delivery is easy.

What I do not know

  • What the best experience for this journey looks like. This is for the research to find out.
  • Who the real users are in Vietnam, and how well the age and lifestyle assumptions above hold.
  • How the app handles discovering new restaurants and rewarding loyal customers today.
  • Which payment methods the app offers in Vietnam and how many steps they need.
  • Which problems users actually meet in the three functions: finding and ordering, checking out and paying, and tracking the order.

Source rules

This is an academic paper. Every claim needs a valid, current source or your own data. The AI follows these rules in every step.

  1. Every claim has a source. Definitions, method descriptions, theory and statements about users need a citation. Results and findings come only from your own data: your walk-through of the app, your evaluation and your usability tests.
  2. Citation style. APA 7 with page numbers, at most two pages per reference, as the brief requires.
  3. Valid sources. Use peer-reviewed articles, established textbooks, recognised standards and primary sources. Do not cite AI tools, blog posts of unclear origin or anonymous pages. The university's study materials are supporting material only and are not cited directly.
  4. Current sources. Prefer recent work. A foundational work, such as the original source of a method, may be older, but mark it as foundational. To confirm: the oldest publication year you accept for other sources.
  5. No inventing. The AI never makes up a citation, a page number, a quote, a figure or a participant result. If it does not know, it says so.
  6. When unsure, ask. If the AI cannot verify a source, a year, a page number or a claim, it asks you and marks the item to confirm. You confirm it against the original before it goes into the paper.
  7. The AI may suggest sources to look for, not sources to trust. You open every suggested source, read the relevant pages and check the details yourself.

Research plan

How the case study will analyse the user guidance of a food delivery app: three core functions, the methods for each, the usability test set-up, the data rules and the open decisions to confirm. Draft for Alternative C, Process Mapping.

⚠ This document is Draft: content has not been verified.

Status: draft. The app, country, language, device and three functions are decided. Everything still marked To confirm is a proposal you decide before you start phase 3.

1. Purpose

The module asks for a case study that reconstructs and analyses an existing user experience, evaluates it, and ends in recommendations. For Alternative C, the focus is user guidance: how well the app leads people through its key processes.

Main question: How well does the food delivery part of the app guide users through finding, paying for and receiving an order, and what would improve it?

Sub-questions:

  1. Where do the three core processes have unnecessary steps, unclear decisions or likely breakdowns?
  2. Which parts of each process cost users the most mental effort?
  3. How do users feel and what do they expect at each touchpoint across the whole journey?
  4. Which usability problems do experts find, and which do real users actually hit?
  5. Which changes would make the guidance clearer, faster and more supportive?

2. Case study

AppGrab, food delivery part only. Decided. Grab is a large provider and the brief prefers a medium-sized one, so justify the choice in the introduction
Why this appIt gives access to a real service, it has more than three connected functions (search, ordering, payment, tracking, chat with the driver), and it is widely used in Asia
Country and languageVietnam, with the app and the tests in English. Decided. Vietnam makes it easier to recruit participants and run the tests. State the country, language and app version in the paper. Check that the Vietnam version of the app offers an English interface
DeviceThe mobile app on an iPhone. Decided. App version recorded: v33437965. To confirm: the exact iPhone model and iOS version, and use the same phone for all screenshots so the screens match
Out of scopeRide-hailing, parcel delivery, the payments wallet as its own product, and the merchant or driver apps

Heuristic evaluation

  • Evaluator: you. A single evaluator finds fewer problems than several, so state this as a limit in the paper. If you can, ask one more person to do a second pass.
  • Rate each issue by severity on a simple scale (for example from 0, not a problem, to 4, usability catastrophe) and write down which heuristic it breaks.
  • Record the screen, the step in the flow and a screenshot for every issue.

Cognitive walkthrough

For each step in each flow, answer the standard questions: will the user try to achieve the right effect, will they notice the right action is available, will they connect the action with their goal, and will they see that progress was made after acting. Mark every step where the answer is no.

GoalSee where real users struggle in the three functions and how the findings compare with the expert evaluation
ParticipantsTo confirm. Proposal: a small group of regular food delivery users. A group of about five is a common choice for this kind of qualitative test, but verify the claim in a citable source before you rely on it. Include some people who know Grab and some who do not, if you can
RecruitingFriends, classmates or colleagues who order food online. No names in the paper
FormatOne-to-one session of about 30 minutes, think-aloud, on the participant's own phone or yours (To confirm)
ConsentShort written or spoken consent: purpose, voluntary, recording, anonymous, can stop any time

Tasks

One scenario per function, in plain words, without telling the user which buttons to press:

  1. You want dinner for two tonight. Find a restaurant that suits you and put your meal in the cart.
  2. You are ready to order. Pay in the way you normally would and place the order.
  3. Your order is on its way. Find out when it will arrive. Then imagine something is wrong with it and find out how to get help.

Real orders cost money. To confirm: stop each session before the final confirmation, or let participants use their own accounts and order for real with a small budget you cover.

What to measure

  • Task success: completed, completed with help, or failed.
  • Time on task.
  • Errors and wrong turns, noted by the observer.
  • One easy rating after each task (for example how easy it felt, on a 7-point scale).
  • Overall impression and quotes at the end of the session.

Procedure

  1. Welcome, explain the test, get consent.
  2. Short questions about their food delivery habits.
  3. The three tasks, with think-aloud.
  4. Rating after each task.
  5. Closing questions: what was easiest, what was most annoying, what would you change.

  5. Write one recommendation for each key problem, and say which evidence supports it.

Effort rating: low means the step is quick and clear, medium means the user must think or compare, high means the step takes a lot of time or attention. All ratings are assumed.

What the user expects from the app

These come from my own understanding, in this order of importance:

  1. Find food easily and order it easily. Finding and ordering is the most important function.
  2. Clear information and a clear preview of what they will get.
  3. A visible reputation for each restaurant, and a delivery time.
  4. Checking out and tracking the order are secondary, but should not get in the way.

In Vietnam, many people still like to pay in cash on delivery, while others use an e-wallet, a Visa or Mastercard, or a bank account. Many users live in houses or apartment buildings, so the way of receiving the order should be flexible for the user to choose.

Principle checks

Written when the Define stage starts. This is the set of principles used in Heuristic Evaluation: checking a design against Nielsen's 10 usability heuristics. Turn each principle into a yes/no question for your product. Add your organisation's own principles below, numbered from P11.

How to use

  1. Write a yes/no question for your product in the last column of the table below. Each principle in the "Details" part has suggested questions: pick, adapt them to your product, then write them into the table.
  2. Evaluate the design: for each decision or screen, go through every question. An answer of "no" is a violation.
  3. Record each violation with its severity (0 to 4), using Severity levels.
  4. Evaluate independently, then merge: one person usually finds only part of the problems. If you can, ask 3 to 5 people to evaluate separately, then merge the results.
  5. Link back to the hub: a decision that passes gets follows pointing at the principle; a decision that violates it and that you accept gets pulls_against and the reason (see ../toolkit/guides/03-how-the-hub-works.md).

Details of the 10 heuristics

Each entry has four parts: It means (an explanation in plain words), Ask when evaluating (yes/no questions to copy into the table above), Signs of a violation, and Example.

P1 · Visibility of system status

It means: the system always tells users what is happening, through timely and visible feedback. Users do not have to guess whether their action took effect.

Ask when evaluating:

  • After each action, does the user see feedback right away (saved, sent, loading)?
  • For anything that takes more than a few seconds, is progress or a "working" state shown?
  • Does the user know where they are in a multi-step process (which step out of how many)?
  • Is the current state of an item (selected, off, confirmed) visible without opening it?

Signs of a violation: pressing a button and nothing happens; a blank page while loading; users pressing again and again because they are not sure the first press worked.

Example: a progress bar while uploading a file; a "Saved" label after pressing Save; a "Step 2 of 4" indicator at the top of a form.

P2 · Match between system and the real world

It means: the system speaks the users' language: words, concepts and an order that are familiar to them, not the system's internal jargon.

Ask when evaluating:

  • Do labels, buttons and messages use words that real users use (no internal terms, error codes, technical names)?
  • Does the order of the steps follow the natural order of what the user is doing in real life?
  • Do icons and metaphors resemble things the user already knows?
  • Does information appear in the user's logic (for example by trip) rather than by the system's data structure?

Signs of a violation: users ask "what does this word mean?"; labels taken from database table names; unexplained abbreviations.

Example: "Shopping cart" instead of "Selected items list"; saved credit cards laid out like physical cards.

P3 · User control and freedom

It means: users often press the wrong thing. They need a clearly marked "emergency exit" to leave an unwanted state without going through a long process.

Ask when evaluating:

  • Can the user cancel or go back at every step of a process?
  • Is there undo for actions that are easy to get wrong?
  • Can the user leave an open window or mode without losing the work in progress?
  • Can a delete or an overwrite be reversed, or is there a confirmation first?

Signs of a violation: no Cancel button; the only way out is to close the whole app; one wrong delete is gone for good.

Example: a "Deleted. Undo" message that shows for a few seconds; a Back button that keeps the data already entered.

P4 · Consistency and standards

It means: the same thing looks and works the same everywhere. Users should not have to wonder whether two different words or actions mean the same thing. Follow the conventions of the platform and the industry.

Ask when evaluating:

  • Does the same action have the same name and the same position on every screen?
  • Does the same component (button, input, card) have the same shape and behaviour?
  • Does the product follow the platform's conventions (iOS, Android, web) for familiar things?
  • Does a word always refer to the same concept?

Signs of a violation: "Save" on one screen but "Confirm" on another for the same job; two different kinds of primary button; an icon that means one thing but is used for two jobs.

Example: the primary button is always at the bottom right and the same colour; the logo at the top left leads to the home page.

P5 · Error prevention

It means: even better than a clear error message is a design that keeps the error from happening. Remove the situations that invite errors, or check and ask for confirmation before the user commits.

Ask when evaluating:

  • For a high-stakes action (delete, pay, send), is there a confirmation step or a way to undo?
  • Does the input help the user enter the right thing (a format hint, sensible defaults, limits)?
  • Does the system block invalid choices instead of letting the user choose and then reporting an error?
  • Are two easily confused actions placed far apart, or clearly different in form?

Signs of a violation: letting the user enter a date in the past and only then saying "invalid date"; a Delete button right next to Save, in the same style.

Example: a date picker that disables the dates that cannot be chosen; a Delete button in a warning colour that asks for confirmation.

P6 · Recognition rather than recall

It means: reduce the load on memory. Put options, information and actions right in front of the user so they can recognise them, instead of forcing them to remember from the previous screen.

Ask when evaluating:

  • Is the information needed for a step shown at that step (the user is not made to remember it from an earlier one)?
  • Can the user pick from a list instead of retyping something from memory?
  • Are actions and options visible instead of hidden behind invisible gestures?
  • If there is history or suggestions (recently used items, autofill), can the user use them?

Signs of a violation: being made to retype a code seen on the previous screen; a function reachable only through a hidden gesture with no hint.

Example: showing recent searches; an order summary next to the payment step.

P7 · Flexibility and efficiency of use

It means: the system serves both newcomers and experienced users. There are shortcuts, customisation and fast ways for frequent users, without confusing newcomers.

Ask when evaluating:

  • Do experienced users have shortcuts (keyboard shortcuts, quick actions, bulk actions)?
  • Does the system remember repeated choices, settings or data so they do not have to be redone?
  • Can newcomers follow the full, clear path?
  • Can users customise the parts they use often?

Signs of a violation: long-time users go through the same five steps every time; no way to act on many items at once.

Example: keyboard shortcuts for common actions; "buy again" from a previous order; multi-select.

P8 · Aesthetic and minimalist design

It means: the interface does not carry extra or barely relevant information. Every additional piece of information competes for attention with the important information and makes it harder to see.

Ask when evaluating:

  • Does each screen have one clear main job, and does it stand out from the rest?
  • Does every element on the screen serve what the user is doing?
  • Is the text short, and is rarely used information tucked away (expandable when needed)?
  • Does decoration slow the user down or make reading harder?

Signs of a violation: a screen full of text and buttons of equal weight; banners, popups and icons competing with each other; long explanatory text nobody reads.

Example: a checkout screen that keeps only the necessary fields; advanced features behind "More options".

P9 · Help users recognise, diagnose and recover from errors

It means: error messages use plain language (no error codes), say precisely what the problem is, and constructively suggest a solution.

Ask when evaluating:

  • Is the error message written in plain words, without error codes or technical terms?
  • Does it say what is wrong and where (pointing to the exact field or step)?
  • Does it say what the user needs to do next, and is there a button to do it?
  • Is the work the user already entered kept so they can fix it without starting over?

Signs of a violation: "An error occurred. Code 500."; an error shown in red at the top of the page without saying which field is wrong; clearing the whole form after an error.

Example: "This email is already in use. Sign in or use another email." with a Sign in button.

P10 · Help and documentation

It means: it is best when the system can be used without explanation. But when help is needed, it must be easy to find, tied to the task at hand, give concrete steps and not be too long.

Ask when evaluating:

  • Can the user find help at the right time and in the right place (in context)?
  • Is the help content searchable and organised by what the user wants to do, not by feature structure?
  • Does it have short, concrete, easy-to-follow steps?
  • For things the user meets for the first time, is there a short hint (onboarding, tip)?

Signs of a violation: help is one long PDF; you have to leave the screen you are on to find it; there is no way to search.

Example: a "?" icon next to a confusing field that opens a one-line explanation; a help-centre search by task.

  • Nielsen, J. (1994). How to Conduct a Heuristic Evaluation. Nielsen Norman Group.

Severity levels

The scale for how serious a usability problem is, used when you evaluate a design (Heuristic Evaluation) or record findings from a test with users. Based on Nielsen's 0 to 4 scale. Use the same scale for every finding so the levels can be compared with each other.

The 0 to 4 scale

LevelNameMeaningExampleWhat to do
0Not a problemYou do not agree this is a usability problemOne evaluator finds a label slightly odd, but real users understand it right awayRecord it and drop it, with the reason
1CosmeticA matter of appearance only, it does not get in the way of the user's workTwo icons are a few pixels out of line; capitalisation of labels is inconsistentFix only if there is time
2MinorA small problem: annoying or a little slow, the user still completes the taskA button is a bit far away so the user has to scroll; an error message does not say clearly how to fix itLow priority; put it in the backlog
3MajorA big problem: many users get blocked, lose a lot of time or have to take a detourCannot find where to share; has to re-enter data after an errorImportant, high priority; fix before launch, or record clearly why it is accepted
4CatastropheSo serious that the user cannot complete the main task, or it causes lost data or lost moneyThe pay button does not work; an accidental delete that cannot be undoneMust be fixed before release

How to decide a level

A problem is rated on three factors, looked at together, not added up:

FactorQuestionLeans toward a higher level when
FrequencyHow often does this problem happen? How many people meet it?Most users meet it, or meet it in the main task
ImpactWhen they meet it, is it easy or hard to get past?They are blocked, lose data, or do not know how to continue
PersistenceIs it met once, or again and again?Users cannot learn to avoid it and meet it every time

For example: a rare error that wipes out all the data can be level 4. An annoyance that happens on every visit but costs one second can be level 2.

How to rate

  1. Rate after you have seen the whole design, not halfway through. You need the overall picture to compare problems with each other.
  2. Each person rates separately, then compare. A single rater is often off; the average of 3 or more people is more reliable.
  3. Rate the problem, not the solution. Severity is about the impact on users, not about how easy it is to fix.
  4. Write a short reason for levels 3 and 4, so others understand why.

Using this scale in the hub

  • Each finding is a file in insights/findings/ with the properties severity (0 to 4) and principle (see Principle checks). The report ranks findings by level by itself, and the Findings page in Stages shows the list.
  • At the Deliver gate, check 4b requires findings to be ranked by importance: use this scale.
  • Level 4 blocks handoff: the report warns while a level 4 finding still has resolution: open, and flags the decision that is affected.
  • Level 3 needs a clear decision: fix it, or accept it as a trade-off and record it in insights/project-log.md.
  • Levels 0 to 2 can go into the backlog after launch.
